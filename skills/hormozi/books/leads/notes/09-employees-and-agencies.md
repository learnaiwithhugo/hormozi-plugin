# Employees and agencies

Two of the four lead getters. Employees run your advertising on your behalf; agencies teach you skills you keep.

---

# Employees

> "the most valuable chapter in the book" (p197, his own framing)

## Why they make you wealthy (p201-202, figure p201)
Two businesses, both $5,000,000 revenue and $2,000,000 profit:

| | Overworked You | Wealthy You |
|---|---|---|
| Hours | 80/week | 10/week |
| Belief | "No one can do it like me" | "Others do it better than me" |
| Income | $2,000,000 | $2,000,000 |
| Net worth | cash | cash **+ $10,000,000 in equity** |

**Why:** a business that only makes money with you in it is a bad investment for anyone else. One that runs without you is an **asset**. $2,000,000 of climbing profit could easily be worth **$10,000,000+ right now.**

> "learning how to get other people to do it for you makes a $10,000,000 difference to your net worth." (p202)

> "You get rich from what you make. You become wealthy from what you own." (p202)

## The beliefs to replace (p202-203)
Out: *If you want it done right, you gotta do it yourself* · *No one can do it like I do it* · *Nobody can replace me.*

His honest diagnosis of why his early hires were bad (p203): **"First, I didn't have the skills to train or lead a team properly. Second, I was too poor, and then (when I had a little money) too cheap to hire anyone better. In other words, it was my fault they sucked."**

The replacements (p203):
> "If you want it done right, get someone to spend all their time doing it."
> "If I can do it, someone else can do it better."
> "Everyone is replaceable, especially me."

> "My suggestion: replace yourself as soon as you can. Then, you can make yourself useful somewhere else." (p203)

## Getting employee leads - the Internal Core Four (p204-205, figure p204)
**The Core Four works for hiring, unchanged.** Reframe from "letting potential customers know about your stuff" to "letting potential employees know about your stuff." (p204)

| Customers | → | Employees |
|---|---|---|
| Warm outreach | → | Asking your network |
| Cold outreach | → | Recruiting |
| Post content | → | Posting job openings |
| Paid ads | → | Promoting job postings |
| Customer referrals | → | Employee referrals |
| Affiliates | → | Associations, guilds, listservs |
| Agencies | → | Staffing firms |
| Employees | → | Employees (unchanged) |

> "when you need to get new talent, you just advertise to get it. And when you need more, you do more." (p205)

Note the corollary: some people are good at hiring and bad at getting customers. **It's the same skill either direction.**

## Training - the 3Ds (p205-206)
**Document → Demonstrate → Duplicate.**

1. **Document.** Write a checklist of the steps exactly as you do them. Have trusted observers watch and document. Bonus: record yourself doing it multiple ways across multiple shifts, so you can watch as an observer rather than breaking flow to take notes. Then **run your next work block following only those steps.** Can you do an A+ job following your own directions exactly? Then you have draft one.
2. **Demonstrate.** You do it in front of them, walking the checklist step by step. Every time they stop you or slow you down to understand something, **adjust the checklist.** That's draft two.
3. **Duplicate.** They do it in front of you, following the same checklist, you observing. If the checklist is right the outcome is the same. If it's off, you find out fast. Fix until it's right, then have them follow it until they get it right.

**The clarity standard (p206):** "if you vanished tomorrow, could a stranger get the results you get if they only followed your checklist?"

**Give short windows to prove themselves (p206).** "Most entry level advertising jobs aren't complex. It takes grit more than skill. If you trained someone properly and they're still below expectations three weeks in, cut them."

**Training notes worth keeping (p206-207):**
- **If they get it wrong or get confused, *we* got it wrong or made it confusing.** If you have to explain what a step means, the step is too complicated - or, more likely, you crammed multiple steps into one.
- If they only "get it" after a long explanation or multiple demonstrations, there's work to do on the checklist. Owners who ignore this get **chronic training problems**.
- **Competence vs performance.** They can know exactly what to do and not be good at it yet. Then the instructions are fine and they just need reps. *"slow then smooth then fast."*
- **Focus on their ability to follow directions more than whether they get the right result.** If they follow directions and get the wrong result, you know it's the *directions* - and you have far more control over that.
- Praise every step done right. **Even when they follow directions exactly and get the wrong result - still praise them for following directions**, then fix the checklist on the spot.
- **Avoid punishment or penalties of any type during training.** Learning a new skill is punishing enough.
- **One piece of feedback at a time**, one step at a time.
- **Whenever there's a major dip from normal performance, retrain the team.** They stopped doing an important step, usually because they didn't know it was important.

## The returns math (p208)
- **Total payroll ÷ total engaged leads = cost per engaged lead**
- Ex: $100,000 ÷ 1,000 leads = **$100 per engaged lead**
- 1 in 10 engaged leads becomes a customer → **$1,000 CAC**
- $4,000 LTGP ÷ $1,000 CAC = **4:1**

**His own number at Acquisition.com (p208):** ~30,000 engaged leads per month, **no paid ads, no outreach.** The content team costs ~$100,000/month. That's **$3.33 per engaged lead** in payroll.

## Which employees to focus on (p208-209)
Same rule as paid ads: if CAC is within 3x industry average, you're good enough - work on LTGP. Above 3x, you have a sales problem or an advertising problem. **The single diagnostic question:**

> **"Do my engaged leads have the problem I solve and the money to spend?"**
> - **No** → they're not qualified → **advertising problem**
> - **Yes**, and they're buying but there aren't enough → **advertising problem**
> - **Yes**, and they're qualified but not buying → **sales problem**

> "Don't fire your sales guy if you've got advertising problems. And equally, don't fire your advertising employees if you've got a sales problem." (p209)

## The hiring philosophy (p209)
> "I believe anyone can be taught to do 'ground level' jobs for any business - advertising or otherwise. So who you pick is not as important as how you train the ones you do."

> "this isn't about brains, it's about guts... nobody is born with an iron will (after all, we all come out crybabies)." Having guts is a **skill**, which means anyone can learn it.

**Get picky only for expensive hires.** For low-level jobs there's never a labour shortage; save the pickiness for hyper-specific multiple-six-figure C-suite hires. **"hire and train anyone willing. Then, when you find winners, and with this method you will: treat them well, don't burn them out, and give them what they deserve."**

# Agencies

**Agencies are lead-getting service businesses.** You pay them to run paid ads, do outreach, or package and distribute content. (p213)

## The trap (p213)
His first agency experience went well. The next ten-plus went like this, every time:
1. They got him excited about all the new leads
2. Onboarding that felt valuable (and sometimes was)
3. They assigned their "best" senior rep
4. He saw some results
5. **They moved the senior rep to the newest customer**
6. A junior rep took over. Results suffered
7. He complained
8. The senior rep came back once in a while to make him feel better
9. Results still suffered. He cancelled
10. He searched for another agency and repeated the cycle
11. Wondered for the zillionth time why he wasn't getting first-time results

> "I actually used an agency the right way... the first time!" (p214)

## When to use one (p214)
**Good agencies cost money.** No money → agencies are out, learn by trial and error.

With money, use them for **exactly two things:**
1. **Learning new methods**
2. **Learning new platforms**

> "Hiring an agency is all about investing in important skills you can't really learn anywhere else." (p214)

## How he uses them now - the script (p215)
> "I want to do what you do in my business, but I don't know how. I'd like to work with you for 6 months so I can learn how you do it. Plus, I'll pay extra for you to break down why you make the decisions you do and the steps you take to make them. Then, after I get a good idea of how it all works, I'll start training my team on it. And once they can do it well enough, I'd like to change to a lower cost consulting arrangement. This way, you can still help us if we run into problems. **Are you opposed to this?**"

Most agencies aren't opposed. If it doesn't work for them, move on - **but be willing to negotiate first.**

**The two-agency play (p215).** Learning YouTube, he hired **two**: one "good enough" agency to keep him committed and do legwork on the platform, and a second **at 4x the price** to really teach the in-depth ideas. Once their videos beat the agency's videos, they dropped to consulting only.

> "I hire one 'good enough' agency to learn the ropes of a new platform. Then, I hire a more elite agency to learn how to maximize it - and I cannot recommend this strategy enough." (p215)

**Why the deadline matters (p215):** "you only get a fraction of the agency's attention, so results get worse whenever they get new clients. Meanwhile, your team gets better and better because they stay focused on you full-time. **So compare your team's results to the agency's until you beat them.** Then, cancel the relationship and put the money into scaling everything you just learned."

## How to pick one - ten signals (p216-217, figure p216)
1. **Somebody you know got good results with them.** If you only know them from their own ads or cold outreach, they probably aren't as good as the ones running on word of mouth - **and the best ones do**
2. Prominent companies you recognize got good results with them
3. **A waiting list** - demand exceeding supply
4. A clear sales process that sets **realistic** expectations
5. **No short-term hacks.** Long-term strategy, with clear timelines for setup, scaling and results
6. They tell you exactly what they need from you, when, and how they use it
7. They suggest a regular meeting schedule and several ways to update you
8. Updates in simple terms with clear tracking, so you can compare costs to results
9. **They make a good offer** - dream outcome, perceived likelihood (how many people like you have they gotten there), time delay, effort and sacrifice (what will you have to do and give up, and can you sustain it)
10. **They are expensive.** "All good agencies are expensive... but not all expensive agencies are good."

**Talk to five or ten agencies before buying (p218).** At first you learn a bunch of new stuff; over time the difference between the good and bad becomes obvious. And if you like the people but they don't fit, **ask them to refer you** - a good specialist agency will send you to other good agencies. "Those are some of my favorite referrals."

## The economics (p218)
The story that opened the chapter: **eight hours and $6,000 to learn a skill that's made him millions.**

To make it work at scale you have to accept **paying the agency and your team to do the same thing for a while.** Yes it costs a lot. Yes it's worth it when you get it right.

**His own learning curve (p218):** first time it took about a year to get his team better than an agency. Then ten months. Then eight. **Now under six months**, every time he wants a new method or platform.

## Next steps (p219)
1. Decide if an agency makes sense right now
2. Talk to a lot of agencies to get a feel for the market. **Don't be cheap**
3. Use the agreement framework above
4. Set a clear deadline to force you *and your team* to learn the skills
5. Run both teams until yours beats theirs regularly
6. Switch to discounted consulting until you feel like you're teaching them - then cut them loose
