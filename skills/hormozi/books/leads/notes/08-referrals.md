# Referrals

**The lowest cost, highest profit, best quality leads there are.** (p180)

**The accidental discovery (p178-179).** Facebook shut off all his ads. He didn't notice for two weeks. Sales dipped from 21 to 15 a week - **and the business was still doing $500,000 a week from word of mouth alone.** A few months later, on stage in front of 700+ gym owners who'd each paid $42,000 to be there, he asked who'd heard about Gym Launch from another gym owner. **Almost the entire room raised their hands.**

> "the best advertising is a happy customer. An amazing product turns every customer into a lead getter." (p180)

## Why they matter, in numbers (p180-181, figure p181)
1. **Worth more (higher LTGP).** Referrals buy more expensive stuff, buy more times, and tend to pay cash up front.
2. **Cost less (lower CAC).** A customer who sends you a customer costs nothing.

**The compounding effect (p181):** at a 4:1 LTGP:CAC you spend 25% of one customer's lifetime gross profit to get another. If every customer brings you **two** more, you're at **12:1** - 8.3% of LTGP per customer. **Three customers for the price of one.**

**And it's exponential, not linear (figure p181).** The Core Four is linear: double the reach outs, roughly double the leads. Word of mouth: one brings two, two bring four, four bring eight. **1... 3... 9... 27... ∞**

## The referral growth equation (p182)
> **% monthly referral rate − % monthly churn = monthly growth**

- **Referrals > churn:** you grow with no other advertising
- **Referrals = churn:** you need other advertising to grow
- **Referrals < churn:** you advertise just to break even - **most people**

> "small businesses barely scrape by because they have about the same customers exiting as they do entering. A hamster wheel of death." (p182)

This is how PayPal and Dropbox exploded into multibillion dollar businesses. (p182)

## Why most businesses get no referrals - two reasons (p183)
**1. The product isn't as good as they think it is.**
> "'Everyone loves our stuff, we just need to get the word out!' - says every small business owner with a product that's not as good as they think it is." (p183)

> "If your product were exceptional, people would already know about it and you'd have more business than you could handle." (p183)

His diagnostic question: **"Why are my customers too embarrassed to tell everyone they know about my product?"** It may be okay - but **unremarkable, as in, not worthy of remark.**

**2. They don't ask.**

## Goodwill - the mechanism (p183-184, figure p183)
> **Price is what you charge. Value is what they get. The difference between price and value is goodwill.** (p184)

Lots of goodwill → word of mouth → referrals.

Two ways to build it: **lower the price, or give more value.** Lowering price is at best temporary - you can only lower it so far for so long. Rory Sutherland: *"Any fool can sell something for less."*

**So the question is never how do we lower our price. It's how do we give more value.**

---

## Six ways to get referrals by giving more value (p184-191)
They map onto the parts of an ad. (p184)

### 1. Call Outs → Sell Better Customers (p185, figure p185)
Better customers get the most value → most goodwill → most likely to refer.

**The case study (p185).** A portfolio company doing PR for generic small businesses: lots of sales, lots of churn, plateaued for years. They looked at the **lowest-churning** customers for a common thread - all in one specific niche, all raising funding from investors. The founder's worry: those were only **15% of his business**, so retargeting risked losing 85%. But the business wasn't growing anyway. He changed the callouts to that narrower "perfect fit" customer.

**Result:** broke through the plateau, grew for the first time in years, on track to add millions per month. **Cost of advertising went down** (more specific messaging = cheaper leads), those cheaper leads got **more value** from a product now meant for them, and **they started referring like clockwork.**

**Action:** figure out what your most successful customers have in common. Target that. Then sell **only** people who meet the new criteria.

### 2. Dream Outcome → Set Better Expectations (p186, figure p186)
> "The fastest, easiest, and cheapest way to make your product remarkable - make it better than they expect. And that's easier than you might think **because you set the expectations.**" (p186)

**Underpromise / overdeliver beats overpromise / underdeliver.** The movie analogy: a stranger says it's awesome, you're disappointed; a stranger says it's terrible, you think it wasn't that bad.

**Action (p186):** "Slowly lower the promises you make when making offers. **Keep lowering them until your close rates lower. At that point, stop.**" That maximizes both customers *and* goodwill.

### 3. Perceived Likelihood of Achievement → Get More People Better Results (p187, figure p187)
Two steps ago you found out *who* the best customers were. Now find out *what they did.*

**Gym Launch (p187):** they tracked speed to first paid ad, speed to first sale, call attendance. Then compared average customers to best customers. **If a gym owner ran paid ads and made a sale in the first seven days, their LTGP tripled.** So they focused everyone on launching ads and making sales in the first seven days. Average results skyrocketed. More customers, testimonials and referrals followed.

**The six-step process (p187):**
1. Survey customers to find who got the best results
2. Interview them to find what they did differently
3. Look at the actions they had in common
4. **Force** new customers to repeat those actions
5. Measure the improvement in average customer results (speed and outcome)
6. **Match the conditions of your guarantee to the actions that get the best results**

**Pro tip (p188), flagged "DO NOT DO THIS IF YOU HATE MONEY AND HELPING PEOPLE":** guarantee new customers those results, **on the condition they do what the best customers did.** The guarantee sells more people; the conditions get them better results.

### 4. Time Delay → Make Faster Wins (p188-189, figure p188)
A "win" is any positive experience. Faster wins increase perceived speed, likelihood they stick, and trust. **Triple win.**

**The mechanism:** you can't compress the work, so give wins **more often.** Same week of progress, delivered as daily updates, is seven times the wins - and seven promises kept makes referring lower risk.

Five ways (p189):
1. Seven small things to deliver → deliver at **shorter intervals**, not all at once
2. **Updates are wins.** Share progress as frequently as possible. "You can never give someone too much good news."
3. Customers form their lasting impression **within the first forty-eight hours** after buying. Force as many wins as you can in that window.
4. **BAMFAM: Book-A-Meeting-From-A-Meeting.** They should always know when they'll next hear from you. Never leave a customer in no man's land.
5. **Never expect customers to forgive you.** Deliver early, never late. He adds **50% to his timelines** so "on time" for him is early for them.

### 5. Effort & Sacrifice → Keep Making Your Stuff Better (p190, figure p190)
Six-step loop, run **monthly** as a recurring process (p190):
1. Use customer service data, surveys and reviews to find the most common problem
2. Figure out the fix - get a headstart from customers who made it work **despite** the problem
3. Use that feedback to improve the product
4. Give the new version to a **small group of struggling customers**
5. Get the next round of feedback; if solved, roll out to everyone, if not, back to step 2
6. Move to the next most common problem and repeat

### 6. Call To Action → Tell Them What To Buy Next (p191)
> "If you have an amazing product, they'll want more. You have to satisfy their desire to buy. If you don't, they'll still buy... but from someone else." (p191)

Weight loss example: customers who didn't refer to the first-tier product **did** refer once they bought the more expensive thing. **So keep selling.**

> "people obsess over their front end offers... But then they neglect the back end and customers fall off. And customers that fall off your product are unlikely to refer." (p191)

## One Question to Rule Them All (p191-192)
> You've lost all your customers but one. The gods of advertising ban you from the Core Four and decree: all customers must come from this one customer. Violate the terms and they destroy your business and every other business you start, for eternity.
>
> **How would you treat this customer?**

What would make their experience so valuable they'd send all their friends? What results would they need? What would onboarding be? What type of customer would you pick? **Write it out. Then do it.**

## Asking - seven ways (p192-196)
> "Asking for referrals only works when you treat it like an offer." (p192)

The referrals come when you show the value **the customer gets** when they refer.

**Two case studies (p192-193):**
- **Dropbox** gave free storage to the customer *and* their referred friend. **39x'd the business in fifteen months.**
- **PayPal** gave $10 credit to both sides. Reached **a million users in two years, 100 million in six.** Still running today.

Three components to any referral program: **how you give the incentive, what you incentivize with, and how you ask.** The seven combos that worked best (p193-195):

**1. One-Sided Referral Benefit.** "I'd rather pay customers than a platform any day of the week." Pay your **average CAC** to either the referrer or the friend. *Ex: CAC is $200 → ask for an actual three-way introduction (call, SMS or email, not just a name and number), right when they buy → write a $200 check when the friend signs up, or give the friend $200 off.* **Always ask for the spouse and give a household discount.**

**2. Two-Sided Referral Benefits.** What Dropbox and PayPal used. Split your CAC across both parties. *Ex: $500 program, $200 CAC → $100 cash to the referrer, $100 off for the friend, up to 3 friends.*

> **Run your paid ads for free (p194).** "In our service businesses we routinely get an additional 25-30% of sign ups as referrals... if we asked for a referral right when they signed up." Sign 100 clients on a promotion, get another 25-30 from referrals. Above 3:1 LTGP:CAC, **the cash from referrals often covered the cost of the ads.**

**3. Ask right when they buy.** On the contract or checkout page, ask for names and numbers of people they'd like to do this with. **Show them they'll get better results with a friend.**
> A new salesman shattered the ticket-sales records. Asked how: *"I'm doing the same thing as everyone else. I just make sure I ask them who else they'd want to have come with them. Then ask them to introduce me."* **Half his sales were referrals.** (p194)
>
> Script: *"People who do our program with someone else tend to get 3x the results. Who else could you do this program with?"*

**Not "if" but "who" (p194).** Once someone is a customer, don't ask *if* they know someone. Ask **who** they know.

**4. Referrals as a negotiation chip.** They want $400, your price is $500 → give the discount **in exchange for introductions to three friends.** You can ethically charge a different price for the same thing because you changed the terms of the sale.
> *"I can't do anything less than $500 down, but if you make a 3-way text introduction to a few of your friends right now, I'd be happy to cut that initiation fee."*
>
> And when a full-price customer finds out (p195): *"Yea - Stacy got $100 off because she referred three friends. I'm happy to give you $100 if you refer me three friends. Who do you have in mind?"* They either back off or give you three friends. **Win win.**

**5. Referral Events.** Points, credits, dollars or bragging rights for bringing friends, inside an explicit window - **typically one to four weeks.** Sell everyone on the benefits of working with others; use stats to show high success rates and the selfish benefit. Names he uses: "Bring-a-friend," "Spouse Challenge," "Accountability Buddy," "Coach Challenge" (teams of your employees and customers).

**6. Ongoing Referral Programs.** Instead of a limited window, talk about the benefits of doing things with others **all the time** - in free content, outreach, paid ads. A friend saw a **33% boost in total sign ups**; of 1,000,000 ticket buyers to his virtual event, **250,000 were referred.**

**7. Unlockable Referral Bonuses.** Bonuses for people who **1) refer and 2) leave a testimonial.** VIP bonuses, courses, tokens, status, training, merchandise, service levels, premium support, extra hours of service. Works well if you don't like paying cash, and can be two-sided since it costs you less than cash.

## The creativity ceiling (p196)
The combined play: give everyone a **gift card for one-third the cost of their program**, to give to a friend who signs up with them. **Expiry in 7-14 days** to force use. It gives the referrer status - rather than *"hey join my program for $2000 off"* they say **"I got this gift card for $2000. Do you want it? I don't want to waste it."**

Add the three-way introduction, text a picture of the card, and **write the friend's name on it first** - it feels personalized and gives you a legitimate reason to ask for the name.

**PS:** you can also sell the gift cards at 90% off as purchasable gifts (only for friends of customers). The referrer looks like they spent a lot; you get paid to get customers.

**Match the thing you give with the thing you sell (p196).** A t-shirt company giving away t-shirts attracts people who want t-shirts. An IT services business giving away a limited edition t-shirt may attract nobody who wants IT services. *(That's why the gift card works so well.)*

## Why referring is a risk you have to lower (p197)
> "referring is always a risk for the customer. They risk their goodwill with their friend in the hopes of getting more by showing them something cool (your stuff). So customers only refer when they think it's very likely their friend will have a good experience."

So: **add benefits with incentives, and lower risk by building goodwill.** Both halves, in that order.

## Action items (p197)
1. **Figure out your referral percentage and your churn percentage** to set a baseline
2. Implement the six giving-value steps to build goodwill
3. Then capitalize on it with one or more of the seven ways to ask

> "I am compensated tomorrow for the value I provide today." (p197)
