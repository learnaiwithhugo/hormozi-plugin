# Cash and billing

The scattered mechanics that decide whether a Money Model actually produces cash. Small changes, disproportionate money.

## The 30-day target
> "I like to cover my costs of getting a customer within 30 days. Main reason: any business can get interest-free money for 30 days in the form of a credit card." (p13)

The ladder of ambition (p169):
- **Good Money Model:** more profit from a customer than it costs to get and service them in 30 days
- **$100M Money Model:** more profit from *one* customer than it costs to get and service *many*, in 30 days

## Bill in four-week cycles, not months
**The book's own "Highest Value Per Word Note" (p149):**
> "Bill weekly (weekly, every 2 weeks, 4 weeks, 12 weeks etc). Here's why. There are 12 months in a year, but the year has 13 four-week cycles. That's an 8.3% difference. If I offer my thing at '$100 every four weeks' (versus $100 a month) the same number of people buy. But, I make 8.3% more annually. To put this into perspective, if your business has 20% margins, this skyrockets annual profit by 41%. And the best part is, you don't do any more work. Just change a few words."

## Billing cadence changes churn
Profitwell data across **14,000 businesses** (p110):

| Billing frequency | Monthly cancellation rate |
|---|---|
| Monthly (12x/yr) | 10.7% |
| Quarterly (4x/yr) | 5% |
| Annual (1x/yr) | 2% |

> "I already present pricing in order of most cash up front to least. It just so happens this also makes customers more valuable over the long term. So start high (fewer bigger payments) and work your way down." (p110)

## The 3% processing fee
> "Get 3% More Revenue For Five Extra Words. 'Yea, it's $X plus a 3% processing fee.' In my life, I've never had anyone not buy because of a processing fee. But 3% added to your topline for no extra work goes straight to your bottom line. If you run a 10% profit business, and add 3%, you just added 30% to your profit." (p149)

## Two forms of payment, using the fee as the trade
Recurring businesses lose cash to expired cards and insufficient funds. Fix both with one move (p149):
> "I ask them if they want a 3% discount (a pretty standard processing fee). 'Do you want to save the processing fee?... awesome. Give us a second form of payment in case anything happens to the first one.'"

And if they ask why: "We only have the processing fee because it costs us man hours to get new payment information every month from our customers. So if you save us time, we pass the savings onto you."

**Get ACH for the second method if you can** - cheapest way to transact besides cash. (p149)

## Declined payments
- **Align payment dates with paycheck dates.** People get paid every two weeks, so charging on those days raises the chance of the card clearing - and boosts 30-day profit far more than monthly payments. (p108, p109)
- **If a card declines, run it a few more times the same day.** Deposits land at different times. "I often recoup a third of my declined payments by adding this little process." (p110)

## The laws of discounting
Stated repeatedly, and worth keeping in one place:
> "the larger the discount, the more people will take it." (p143)

> "Bigger discounts make you less profit per sale, but they get more sales." (p97)

Which is why the rollover rule exists: **price the next offer at least 4x the credit you're rolling over**, so applying the whole credit discounts by 25% at most. (p97)

## Raise price in stages
> "Make new offers cheap at first. Then, as you get yeses, raise the price. Lots of early yeses get customer feedback and make the product better. Then, as the offer gets reliable, start raising the price. And keep raising the price until you cannot make up for the nos with the extra cash you make from the yeses. In other words, keep raising the price until you make less money." (p166)

## Cash discipline on prepaid work
> "if you get a whole year's worth of payments in a month, make sure you can deliver for the whole year. Budget the correct amount of money to service your customers for the duration of your agreement. Don't be a goon and buy yourself a house with the cash meant for servicing your customers." (p51)

## Continuity vs one-time pricing
The full table sits in [05-continuity.md](05-continuity.md) (figure p142). The short version: **people pay about 33% more to avoid a subscription**, so a standalone priced 1.33x your monthly gets roughly half taking each. Every extra multiple pushes more of them into continuity, up to 2.66x for 90%.

## Which money to chase, in order
> "First, I get customers reliably then I make sure they pay for themselves reliably then I make sure they pay for other customers reliably then I start maximizing each customer's long-term value then I spend as many advertising dollars as I can to print as much money as possible." (p161)
