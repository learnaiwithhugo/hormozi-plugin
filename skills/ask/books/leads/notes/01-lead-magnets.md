# Lead magnets

**This is the most heavily highlighted chapter in the book: 470 marked words.** When in doubt, this is the material that matters most.

## What a lead magnet is
> "A lead magnet is a complete solution to a narrow problem." (p29)

Typically low-cost or free. Once solved, **it reveals another problem that your core offer solves.** (p29)

The salty pretzel at a bar: the pretzels solve hunger, then reveal a thirst problem the drink solves for money. The pretzels cost you something; the drink revenue covers it and profits. (p29)

**Advertise your core offer first.** "If you advertise your core offer, then you go straight for the sale - the direct path to money. Advertising your core offer might be all you need to get leads to engage. Try this way first." (p28, figure p28) The lead magnet is what you add when people want to know more before buying - common for expensive stuff.

> "A person who pays with their time now is more likely to pay with their money later." (p29)

**Even free stuff has a cost (p29).** "If your lead magnet isn't worth their time, it's overpriced." And: "if they think your lead magnet is worth their time, they'll think your core offer is worth their money."

## The seven steps (p29-30, all highlighted)
1. Figure out the problem you want to solve and who to solve it for
2. Figure out how to solve it
3. Figure out how to deliver it
4. Test what to name it
5. Make it easy to consume
6. Make it darn good
7. Make it easy for them to tell you they want more

Grand Slam Offer logic applies to free stuff as much as paid. Make the free thing so good people feel stupid saying no. (p30)

## Step 1: pick the problem (figure p30)
The **Problem-Solution cycle**: every problem has a solution, every solution reveals more problems, and small cycles sit inside larger ones. (p30)

**Highlighted (p31):** "We start by picking a problem that's narrow and meaningful. Then, solve it." And: "when we solve one problem, a new problem reveals itself." And: if you can solve that new problem with your core offer, you've got a winner.

Worked example (p31, highlighted): helping homeowners sell their homes is a **broad** solution. The narrow problems around it - what's it worth, how to raise the value, pictures, cleaning, landscaping, minor repairs, moving, staging - those are lead magnets. Solve one free. It makes the remaining problem more obvious. Now charge for the rest.

**Action: pick the narrowly defined problem. Then check your core offer solves the next one that comes up.** (p31)

## Step 2: pick the type - three of them (p31-33, figure p31)
**Highlighted (p31):** if the audience has a problem they don't know about, make them aware of it; or solve a recurring problem briefly with a sample or trial; or give one step of a multi-step process.

1. **Reveal Their Problem** - think "diagnosis." Best when the problem gets worse the longer you wait. Website speed test, posture analysis, termite inspection.
2. **Samples and Trials** - full but brief access. Limit uses, time, or both. Best when the core offer is a recurring solution to a recurring problem. Costco samples.
3. **One Step Of A Multi-Step Process** - best when the core offer solves something complex. This book is one. Free first coat of garage sealant, sell the other two.

**Highlighted (p33):** "You give away free finance courses, guides, calculators, templates, etc. They are so valuable people really can do it all themselves." - then you sell them the service, because doing it themselves reveals the time, effort and sacrifice.

**Best case, do all three at once.** Highlighted (p34): "So if you can, try and get a lead magnet that does all three: reveal a problem, give them a taste of the solution, and show it as a small piece of a total package." The dressing room is the perfect example - trial, reveals a problem, and step one of building an outfit. (p34)

**Be a drug dealer (p33, highlighted):** "This is why we don't 'dilute' the value of our lead magnets or give away sucky fluff. If anything, like a drug dealer, you'd wanna give the strongest 'hit' first. It keeps them coming back for more." Your lead magnet is the first hit. The next one they pay for.

## Step 3: pick the delivery (p34-35, figure p34)
**Highlighted (p34):** "my favorite lead magnets solve them with: software, information, services, and physical products."

1. **Software** - give them a tool. Spreadsheet, calculator, dashboard.
2. **Information** - teach them something. Course, lesson, interview, event, mistakes, hacks.
3. **Services** - do work for free. Website audit, first layer of sealant, turn their video into an ebook.
4. **Physical products** - something they hold. Assessment chart, sample, a book.

3 types x 4 delivery methods = **up to twelve lead magnets for a single narrow problem.** Make as many versions as you can and rotate them. Keeps advertising fresh and low effort, and you find out which wins. (p35)

## Step 4: test the name (p35-39, figures p35, p36, p37, p38)
Ogilvy: "When you have written your headline, you have spent 80 cents of your (advertising) dollar." Five times more people read the headline than anything else. (p35)

> "improving the headline, name, and display of your lead magnet can 2x, 3x, or 10x your engagement." (p36)

Test three things **in this order: headline, image(s), subheadline.** If you only test one, test the headline. (p36)

He tested this book's own title (p36-38):
- Advertising **69%** vs Promotion 31%
- Leads **58%** vs Advertising 42%
- Leads **54%** vs Marketing 46%
- "How to get strangers to want to buy your stuff" beat "Get strangers to want to buy your stuff" **67/33** - two words, "how to"
- and beat "How to get *more* strangers..." **71/29** - one word removed

> "Small changes can make big differences." (p38)

**How to run it with no audience:** run polls if you have any following at all. Otherwise post on every platform and ask people to reply "1" or "2" and count them. Otherwise message people and ask. "There's always a way, and this is one of the highest leverage things you can do with your time" (p39)

**Bonus signal:** if people respond to the poll *and* ask when they can get it, you have a mega winner. (p39)

## Step 5: make it easy to consume (p39-40, figure p39)
> "You can see 2x, 3x, and even 4x+ increases in take rates and consumption simply by making it easier to consume." (p39)

Per type: software on phone and computer, multiple formats; information as image, video, text, audio - offer them all; services available more times in more ways; physical products fast to order, fast to open, simple directions.

$100M Offers has a near-even split across ebook, physical, audiobook and video. Multiple formats is "the easiest way I know to get 2-3-4x the amount of leads for the same work." (p40)

## Step 6: make it darn good (p40)
**Give away the secrets, sell the implementation.**

**Highlighted (p40):** "The marketplace judges everything you have to offer - free or not." · "you can never provide too much value. But, you can provide too little." · make it so good "people feel obligated to pay you." · **"make your lead magnets as good as your paid stuff. Your reputation depends on it."**

The alternative is the vicious cycle: **highlighted (p40)** - people who might've become customers think this person only has sucky fluff, buy from someone else, and tell others not to buy from you.

The mechanism: people buy based on how much value they *think* they'll get after buying. The easiest way to make them think that is to give them value **before** they buy. (p40)

> "99% of people aren't gonna buy, but they will create (or destroy) your reputation based on the value of your free stuff." (p40)

## Step 7: the CTA (p41-43)
Good CTAs have two parts: **what to do**, and **a reason to do it right now.** (p41)

**What to do (highlighted, p41):** CTAs tell the audience how to become engaged leads. Clear, simple, direct. Not "don't delay" - "call now."

**Reasons to act now:**
- **Scarcity (p41, highlighted).** Limited amount. The best scarcity strategy he knows is **reality**: if you can't handle more than five new customers a week, say so. Don't keep your real capacity limits secret - advertise them. That's ethical scarcity. **Highlighted:** *"I can only handle five people per week, so if you want it solved soon, do xyz..."*
- **Urgency (p41-42, highlighted).** "Urgency is when people act faster because they have a short amount of time." Deadlines on promotions, bonuses that expire.
- **Any reason at all (p42).** The Harvard line-cutting study: good reasons work better than bad ones, but *any* reason beats no reason. Think of what comes after the word "because." (His favourite: "Fraternity Party Planner (my favorite) - Make Up A Reason.")

**Pro tip (p42):** put the time limit on the **bonus**, not the price. You never change your pricing or products, you just rotate a handful of valuable bonuses weekly. "an easy way to make CTAs more effective without capping sales."

**Highlighted (p43):** "Action Step: Give a clear, simple, action-oriented CTA. Then, give them a reason why using scarcity, urgency, and any other reasons you can think of. And, do it often. Don't be clever, be clear."

## The money math that justifies all of it (p43)
Without a lead magnet:
- $10,000 profit on the core offer
- $1,000 in ads to get someone on a call
- close 1 in 3 → **$3,000 CAC**

With a free lead magnet that costs $25 to deliver:
- more engage, so ads cost $75 per call → **$100 all-in per call**
- 1 in 10 of those buy the core offer → **$1,000 CAC**

**Cost to get a customer cut by 3x. Same ad budget, triple the business.** (p43)

> "With a $25 budget to deliver your lead magnet, you can provide FAR more value than a $0 budget." (p43)

Note: **even a lead magnet that costs money to deliver should lower your CAC.** More engaged leads means more chances at customers, and the extra customers more than cover the cost. That's the point. (p43)

## What a good lead magnet does - the four-point test (p44)
1. Engages ideal customers when they see it
2. Gets more people to engage than the core offer alone
3. Is valuable enough that they actually consume it
4. Makes the right people more likely to buy

## Checklist
- [ ] One narrow, meaningful problem named
- [ ] Core offer solves the *next* problem it reveals
- [ ] Type picked (reveal / sample / step 1 of X) - ideally all three
- [ ] Delivery picked (software / information / service / physical)
- [ ] Headline tested against at least one alternative with real people
- [ ] Available in every format you can manage
- [ ] Good enough that you could charge for it
- [ ] One CTA, with a reason attached, repeated often
