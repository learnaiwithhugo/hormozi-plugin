# Post free content

One-to-many, to people who know you. Warm outreach makes you repeat yourself ten times to reach ten people. Content says it once and reaches all ten. (p75)

**The core insight (p73):** "the content you create isn't the compounding asset - the audience is." Content disappears; the audience keeps growing.

**The volume lesson (p74).** He paid $120,000 for four calls with a big influencer. Call one: post regularly on every platform. Twelve months later: +200,000 people. Call two, asking for the secret blueprint: *"Bro, anyone telling you there's some secret is trying to sell you something. We just put out as much as we possibly can... You just gotta do more bro."* He 10x'd his output; the audience grew 10x as fast, adding 1.2M in six months.

**The trade-offs, stated (p75):** harder to personalize so fewer respond · you compete with everyone else posting · if you stand out, people copy you, so you must constantly innovate.

# Part I: making the content

## The content unit (p76, figure p76)
**Highlighted (p76):** "audience-growing content does one thing - it rewards the people consuming it." And a person can only be rewarded if they **"1) Have a reason to consume it and 2) Pay attention long enough to 3) Get that reason satisfied."**

Reverse those into the three jobs (all highlighted, p76):
- **Hook attention:** get them to notice your content
- **Retain attention:** get them to consume it
- **Reward attention:** satisfy the reason they consumed it to begin with

> "The smallest amount of material it takes to hook, retain and reward attention is a content unit." (p76)

It can be one image, one meme, one sentence - all three jobs at once. That's why short tweets and memes go viral.

## 1) Hook (p77-81, figure p77)
Measured by the **percentage of people who start consuming.** Three levers: topics, headlines, format.

### Topics - the five sources (p77-79, all highlighted)
**Highlighted:** he prefers **personal experiences**, because **"there's only one of you"** - the easiest way to differentiate is to say something nobody else can say.

The five categories (**highlighted, p77**): **Far Past, Recent Past, Present, Trending, Manufactured.**

- **Far Past (highlighted):** "The important past lessons in your life." Connect the wisdom to what you sell. *Give them the story without the scar.* "It's why I write these books."
- **Recent Past (highlighted):** "Do stuff, then talk about what you did (or what happened)." Look at last week's calendar, meetings, conversations, warm reach outs. Testimonials and case studies live here. Requires taking notes and recordings.
- **Present (highlighted):** "Write down ideas at the exact time they come to you." Keep a capture method in arm's reach; pause meetings to note them. He posts ideas publicly as they happen - if one outperforms, he makes more on that topic.
- **Trending (highlighted):** "Go where the attention is." Apply your own experience to what's trending. Very effective for reaching a broader audience.
- **Manufactured (highlighted):** "Turn your ideas into reality. Pick a topic people find interesting. Then, learn about it, make it, or do it. Then, show it to the world." Most time and effort, biggest payouts. *"I lived on $100 for a month."*

**Manufactured vs documenting (p79).** Manufactured has the most upside by far - the top ten videos on the most popular video platform are all music videos, ~60 billion views. But documenting keeps volume high and is more sustainable over a lifetime. A famous creator's line: *"I don't want to be filling my living room with sand when I'm fifty."*

### Headlines - the seven news components (p80, all highlighted)
Include **at least two**:
| Component | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Recency** | as recent as possible, literally the "news" |
| **Relevancy** | personally meaningful (nurses care about nurse news) |
| **Celebrity** | prominent people, authorities |
| **Proximity** | close to home, geographically |
| **Conflict** | opposing ideas, people, nature (pineapple on pizza) |
| **Unusual** | odd, unique, rare, bizarre |
| **Ongoing** | stories still in progress, evolving, plot twists |

### Format (p81)
Match the popular format of the platform. People consume content because it looks like stuff they've liked before.
> "You've gotta make your content look like what they expect will reward them. Otherwise, no matter how good it is, better-looking content will hook them before yours even has a chance." (p81)

Format for the platform first, then tweak for your ideal audience. Use the best content on the platform that targets your market as the guide.

## 2) Retain (p82-83, figure p82)
**Highlighted (p82):** "My favorite driver of retention is curiosity." · "if done correctly, people will wait years. People want to know what happens... next."

Method: **embed unresolved questions in their minds**, explicitly or implicitly. Three vehicles:

- **Lists (highlighted, p82):** "things, facts, tips, opinions, ideas, etc. presented one after the other." Follow a theme. **Highlighted examples: "Top 10 Mistakes"**, **"5 Biggest Money Makers."** Giving the number up front **"tells people what to expect"** and retains attention longer.
- **Steps (highlighted, p83):** "actions that occur in order and accomplish a goal when completed." *"3 Steps to Creating a Great Hook."*
- **Stories (highlighted, p83):** "describe events, real or imaginary." Things that happened, might happen, or never will. All three drive curiosity.

**The difference (highlighted, p83):** steps "are actions that must be done in a specific order to get a result" - less flexible, **more explicit reward.** Lists "can have just about anything on them in any order you want" - more flexible, **less explicit reward.**

Interweave them freely: lists within steps, a story per list item.

**Highlighted (p83):** "Embed questions in their minds to make them want to know what happens next."

## 3) Reward (p84-85, figure p84)
**Highlighted (p84):** "How good your content is depends on how often it rewards your audience in the time it takes them to consume it." Think **value per second** (highlighted).

> "there is no such thing as too long, only too boring." (p84)

You can't guarantee a reward, only raise the chance: hook the right audience, retain with lists/steps/stories, then **clearly satisfy the reason they came.**

Bad rewarding: promising "7 Ways to Make Up with Your Spouse" and giving four ways, or seven bad ones, or delivering it to a room of single guys.

**The scoreboard is not your opinion. Highlighted (p84):** "Here's how you know if you succeeded: your audience grows. If it's not growing, your stuff isn't that good. Practice and you'll get better."

## Short vs long form (p85, figure p85)
There isn't much difference. **Long form is just more content units linked together.** Longer takes more skill because you must string more good units in a row - like a comedian earning an hour instead of a five-minute bit. Start short, build from there.

## The rule most creators break (p86)
**Highlighted (p86):** "you need to make content assuming the people consuming it have never heard of you before." Make it for strangers and strangers will like it, because you made it for them - and they'll share it. Inside jokes make everyone else feel unwelcome and slow your growth. Existing audience won't mind; they appreciate the reminders.

# Part II: monetizing it

## The give:ask ratio (p87-89, figure p88)
**Highlighted (p87):** "You deposit goodwill with rewarding content, then withdraw from it by making offers."

The studied numbers (p88): television runs 13 min of ads per 60 min = **3.5:1**. Facebook is roughly **4 content posts per 1 ad**. Those are *mature* platforms optimizing for money, not growth - so that's the **minimum** sustainable ratio, not the target.

**Highlighted (p88):** "So if you want to grow an audience, give far far more than you ask."

**The strategy he actually recommends: give until they ask.** (figure p88)
**Highlighted (p89):** "If you give enough, people start asking you." · "makes people uncomfortable to continue to receive without giving back."

You get the best customers this way - the biggest givers, who as paying customers still feel they got the better end. And growth never slows. **Highlighted (p89): "Give give give give give give until they ask."**

**Give in public, ask in private (p89).** Never stop giving publicly; let an increasing number of people ask you privately to buy.

> "The moment you start asking for money is the moment you decide to slow down your growth." (p89)

**The cautionary case (p91, highlighted):** a friend's **podcast that blew up quickly**; eager to monetize, **he started making offers** too frequently inside the content. **"His podcast not only stopped growing, it actually shrank!"** Don't kill the golden goose.

## Two ways to ask (p90-92, figures p90, p91)
- **Integrated:** the ask sits inside every piece of content, as long as the ratio stays high. An hour podcast with 3 × 30-second ads = 58.5 min giving to 1.5 min asking. Dominates on **long-form** platforms.
- **Intermittent:** many pure-give pieces, then an occasional ask piece. 10 give posts, an ask on the 11th. Dominates on **short-form** platforms.

**Where to put the integrated ask (p91):** after a valuable moment, or at the end. Add a second only once you've confirmed growth hasn't slowed.

**PS statements (p91):** one of the most-read parts of anything written, because it summarizes what the author wants you to do. One of his favourite places for an ask.

**What you ask for:** your core offer, or your lead magnet. That's it. **If unsure, do the lead magnet - it's lower risk.** (p92)

**One CTA at a time (p95).** 'A confused mind doesn't buy' is a common saying in the sales and marketing world. Don't ask people to "share, like, subscribe, and comment" - they'll do none. Want shares? Only ask for shares.

## Scaling output (p93-94, figure p93)
Two opposing strategies, both right:
- **Depth then width:** maximize one platform, then add the next. *Advantages:* compounding returns on one platform, fewer resources. *Disadvantages:* no omnipresence, and single-channel risk - platforms change rules and ban people for no reason.
- **Width then depth:** get on every relevant platform early, then maximize them together. *Advantages:* broader reach faster, repurposing efficiency. *Disadvantages:* more labour, and people often end up with lots of bad content everywhere.

**Volume ceilings he names (p93):** short form, sometimes **up to ten times per day per platform**; long form, up to **five days per week**.

**His own process (p94):** ~1 hour finding topics using the five topic sources · sit down **twice a month** and record ~30 short clips · same day, record 2-4 longer videos unpacking tweets that had stories or examples.

## Seven lessons (p96-97)
1. **Highlighted:** switch from "How to" to **"How I."** From "This is the best way" to "These are my favorite ways." **"Talk about what you've done, not what others should do."** **Highlighted:** "It's subtle. But when you tell your experience, you are sharing value." Telling a stranger what to do is hard to do without coming off preachy.
2. **Highlighted: "We Need To Be Reminded More Than We Need To Be Taught."** He posts about his book daily; 1 in 5 surveyed still didn't know he had one. **Highlighted:** "Keep repeating yourself. You get bored of your content before your whole audience even sees it."
3. **Puddles, Ponds, Lakes, Oceans.** Narrow the focus. A local plumber shouldn't make general business content - the audience will listen to people with better track records. Be king of the puddle first, then expand.
4. **Content creates tools for salespeople.** Keep a master list of "greatest hits," labelled by the problem solved and benefit provided, for the sales team to send before or after calls.
5. **Free content retains paying customers.** Buyers are *more* likely to consume your free content, and they fold it into their ROI calculation on the paid thing.
6. **Highlighted:** people don't have shorter attention spans, they have higher standards. **"there's no such thing as too long, only too boring"** - people binge long form **"if they like it."**
7. **Avoid pre-scheduling.** Manually posted content performs better; the close feedback loop makes you try harder.

## Benchmarks (p97-98)
Measure monthly: **total followers and reach (how big)** and **rate of growth (how fast)**.
- 1,000 → 1,500 followers in a month = 50% growth month
- 10,000 → 15,000 reach = 50% growth month

> "we can only control inputs. Measuring outputs is only useful if we are consistent with inputs." (p98)

Pick a posting cadence and an ask cadence, then **"Do. Not. Stop."** He posted a podcast twice a week for **four years** before hitting a Top 100 list; it became Top 10 in the US in year five.

**Highlighted (p98):** Dr. Kashey - "The more ways you measure, the more ways you can win." · **"Measure a lot. Adapt to feedback. Be a winner."**

## Why to make content even if it isn't your main channel (p95)
January 2020, paid ads stopped working. The team blamed creative, copy, offer, pages, sales, price. Leila asked what they'd *stopped* doing. Answer: Alex stopped making gym content. He surveyed the gym owners:

> **"78% of all clients had consumed at least TWO long form pieces of content, prior to booking a call."** (p95)

The free content was nurturing the demand paid ads got credit for. **Free content improves the returns of every other advertising method**, even when it's hard to measure.

| | |
|---|---|
| Who | Yourself |
| What | Value: give give give until they ask |
| Where | Any media platform |
| To whom | People who already follow you |
| When | Every morning, 7 days a week |

**Do not swap this for warm outreach - stack it.** Content grows the warm audience, which makes warm outreach better. (p100)
