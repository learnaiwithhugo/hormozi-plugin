# Paid ads: making the ad

One-to-many, to people who don't know you. **You pay another person or business to put your offer in front of their audience.** Renting eyeballs. (p129, figure p129)

**The trade:** you skip the time it takes to build an audience by trading money for reach. **The reach is guaranteed. Getting your money back isn't.** So it's a game of *efficiency*, not reach. (p129)

> "the question isn't 'do ads work?' it's 'how well can you make them work?'" (p129)

> "if an ad isn't profitable, most of the time, it's because the right people never saw it." (p129)

## The whole method in one paragraph (p130)
Start with the entire world (the haystack), then narrow to needles: **pick a platform holding your audience → use the platform's targeting → craft the ad so it repels everyone else → tell whoever's left what to do next.** People overcomplicate it. That's all it is.

**Then expand the puddle.** Once you're profitable in a small audience, go to a pond, a lake, an ocean. Efficiency drops as the audience grows, but total profit goes up: $1,000 → $10,000 ($9k profit) becomes $100,000 → $300,000 ($200k profit). **Ratio down, money up.** (p130)

Four problems to solve (p130): where to advertise · getting the right audience to see it · making the best ad for them · getting permission to contact them.

## Step 1: pick a platform (p131, figure p131)
Four things must be true (p131):
1. **You've used it and gotten value from it as a consumer** - so you know how it works
2. You can target people on it interested in your stuff
3. You know how to format ads specific to it
4. You have the minimum money to place an ad

**Place ads where your competitors place ads, to start (p131).** Platforms have multiple ad types (LinkedIn message vs newsfeed; Instagram feed vs stories; YouTube sidebar, mid-stream or pre-roll). Look at ad placement of others in your space and start there. **"Replicate before you iterate."**

**Start by consuming ads on the platform** as your first step to learning how to make one. (p131)

## Step 2: targeting (p132-133, figure p132)
The first round of targeting was choosing the platform. A 100M-user platform already cuts 99% of the world; excluding non-English speakers takes you to 99.5%. **Specific is good.** (p132)

> "The right message to the wrong audience will fall on deaf ears." (p132)

Two mechanisms, usable separately or combined (p132-133):
1. **Lookalike audience.** Upload a list; the platform finds a bigger, similar audience. **Build it in order of quality:** current and previous customers first → add warm reach out list if too small → add cold reach out leads to hit the minimum. Forcing the list to size can make the lookalike too broad, which is fine because...
2. **Targeting factors.** Age, income, gender, interests, time, location. Nobody over 45 ever bought? Exclude them. Sell car parts? Show ads during car shows and on car channels.

**More filters = more specific = more efficient ads, but you burn through the list faster.** The wins from small specific audiences fund advertising to larger broader ones later. **That is how you scale.** (p133)

**Local targeting (p133):** local markets are already ~0.1% of a nation. **Be as specific as possible, but no further** - don't stack many more filters on a local audience.

**Eventually you'll also have a list of people who gave contact info from ads but didn't buy. That comes in handy.** (p133)

## Step 3: what the ad says - three chunks (p133-144)
> "1) Callouts (for them to notice it) 2) Value Elements (to give them reason to do something) 3) Calls to action (to give them a way to do it)" (p146)

### 3a. Call out (p134-137)
**"People noticing your ad is the most important part of the ad... by a lot."** The purpose of each second is to sell the next second. **"my advertising became 20x more effective when I focused the majority of my effort on the first five seconds."** This is the part he tests most. (p134)

The **cocktail party effect**: in a loud ballroom, one sound cuts through - your name. Callouts harness that. (p134)

**Verbal callouts (p134-135):**
| Type | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Labels** | words putting people in a group - features, traits, titles, places | *Clark County Moms* · *Gym Owners* · *Remote Workers* |
| **Yes-Questions** | if they answer "yes, that's me," they qualify | *Do you wake up to pee more than once a night?* |
| **If-Then Statements** | meet the condition, get the help | *If you run over $100,000/month in ads, we can save you 20%...* |
| **Ridiculous Results** | bizarre, rare, out of the ordinary | *This woman lost 50 pounds eating pizza and fired her trainer* |

**Local is the strongest label (p135):** "LOCAL AREA + TYPE OF PERSON" is one of his all-time favourites. Americans < Texans < Dallas Residents < **Irving Residents.**

**Nonverbal callouts (p135-137):**
- **Contrast** - what sticks out in the first few seconds. Bright shirt > dull shirt. Attractive > plain. Moving > still.
- **Likeness** - *quack like a duck.* Want plumbers? Dress like one, talk like one, be in a plumbing environment. Broad customer base → use more ethnicities, ages, genders, personalities. Narrow → look like them. **Mascots** work because they don't age, ask for money, or take days off. **Advanced:** if the spokesperson isn't you, the business is less dependent on you and therefore more sellable.
- **The Scene** - show the Yes-Question or If-Then. Person tossing in bed → sleep trouble. Room stacked to the ceiling → too much junk. A local landmark → *"Hey, I know that place!"*

**Infinite ads (p137) - the highest-ROI tip in the chapter:** record ~10 new ads per week, but record **30+ first sentences or questions** (five-second clips) to open them. **30 callouts × 10 ads = 300 variations in a matter of hours.** Once you know the best callout, apply it to all ads.

### 3b. Value - the What-Who-When framework (p137-143, figure p138)
> "the best ads make the benefits look as big as possible and the costs look as small as possible." (p137)

**THE WHAT - eight key elements** (the four value drivers and their opposites, p138-140):
| Driver | Its opposite |
|---|---|
| **Dream Outcome** - the maximum benefit they can achieve | **Nightmare** - the worst hassles of going without |
| **Perceived Likelihood of Achievement** - lower the risk with proof, authority, guarantees | **Risk** - how risky it is *not* to act; their past failures repeating and getting worse |
| **Time Delay** - how much faster they get it | **Slow** - how slow their current trajectory is, or that they'll never get there |
| **Effort and Sacrifice** - **Ease**: avoid what they hate, keep what they love, without hard work or skill | **Effort** - the work and skill required *without* you, and the sacrifice they're already making for nothing |

**THE WHO (p140).** Humans are status-driven, and status comes from how other people treat them. So show the value elements **from other people's perspectives**: spouse, kids, parents, extended family, colleagues, bosses, friends, rivals, competitors. Lose weight → do your kids have a new role model? Does your spouse get healthy too? Does your frenemy stop the little jabs at dinner? Risk-free → the spouse won't nag about the purchase.

**THE WHEN (p141).** Run each of those through **past, present and future.** Teased as a kid (past) → struggling to button the jeans (present) → moving up another belt loop (future). And run the timeline through *other people's* perspectives too: the doctor saying he might not walk his daughter down the aisle.

**Put them together and you answer WHY they should be interested.** (p141)

> "The more angles we cover, the more interested they'll become." (p142)
> "the only difference between long ads and short ads is how many angles we have time to cover." Keep the callouts and CTAs the same either way. (p142)

**Specific but no specific-er (p142):** more specific copy is more efficient but longer, and too long for the platform lowers efficiency. If you have audio and visuals, use contrast, likeness and the scene to carry specificity **without adding length.**

**Get unlimited inspiration (p142):** search "[PLATFORM] ad library." **If an ad has run a month or more, assume it's profitable.** Take notes on the callouts, how they illustrate value elements, and their CTAs. **Break down fifty ads** for a massive head start.

**You don't need to become a copywriter (p143):** "If you explain your offer clearly using the What-Who-When framework, you'll have enough skill to remove copywriting as a limiter on your growth... if you call out the right people and have an amazing offer, you barely need any copy to begin with."

### 3c. CTA (p143-144)
Huge motivation, tiny window. **S-P-E-L-L it out:** click this button, call this number, reply "YES," go to this website, scan this QR code.

**Make CTAs quick and easy.** Easy phone numbers, obvious buttons, short memorable web addresses. (He paid $370,000 for Acquisition.com and thinks everyone else *undervalues* easy domains.)

> "Assume the audience has no idea who you are, what you do, how it works, they're in a rush, and they have a 3rd grade education." (p144)

All the scarcity, urgency and bonus tactics from `01-lead-magnets.md` step 7 apply here too.

## Step 4: get permission - the landing page (p144-145, figure p145)
His favourite way to get contact info is a simple landing page. **Three layouts, all: headline → subhead → email → submit.** Pick one and start testing. (p145)

> "The simpler your landing page, the easier it is to test. Focus on the words and the image." (p144)

**Make the landing page match the ad (p145).** They clicked because you promised a benefit - carry the same look and language over. **"You want a continuous experience from 'click to close.'"**

**Consistency drives the second action (p145).** Cialdini: people like to think of themselves as consistent. Remind them of the action they just took and show how the next one aligns. *"Now that you just did A, you need to do B to get the most of A."*

> "we aren't selling anything. We are asking if they're interested in the stuff we sell." (p145)

**On being scared of it (p145):** "I wasted four years feeling too scared to make a landing page. When I finally tried, I finished before lunch."
